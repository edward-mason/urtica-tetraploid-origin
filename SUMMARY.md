# SUMMARY — one page, plain language

**What we asked.** Stinging nettle comes in two forms that look alike but differ in chromosome number: a rarer diploid (26 chromosomes) of natural habitats, and the common tetraploid (52 chromosomes) that follows people, fertiliser and disturbed ground across Europe. Botanists have long supposed the tetraploid arose from a cross between two different nettle lineages (an "allotetraploid"). A 2021 population study found tetraploids and diploids genetically inseparable, but nobody had measured, gene by gene, whether the tetraploid carries a second, divergent genome.

**What we did.** We used only public genomes: a diploid mother and her diploid son from Romania/Bulgaria (each with both haplotypes separated by the original authors), the UK tetraploid sequenced by the Darwin Tree of Life project (deposited as a primary and an alternate pseudo-haplotype), and a German nettle genome released in 2026. Before downloading anything we wrote down the test, its thresholds, controls and what would count as a positive or negative result. For about 13,400 genes we measured how different the tetraploid's gene copies are from the Romanian diploid's and compared that with how different the unrelated Bulgarian diploid's copies are from the same genes. A hybrid origin would leave a large class of genes far more different than any diploid-to-diploid comparison; a within-lineage doubling would not. Two independent reviewers then tried to reproduce and to break the result; everything they asked for was added.

**What we found.** The tetraploid's genes are 1.11 times as different from the Romanian diploid as the Bulgarian diploid's are (95 % interval 1.06–1.16) — essentially the same — and only 1.6 % of genes exceed the diploid's own 99th percentile, where a hybrid would give 20 % or more. The alternate pseudo-haplotype, held out until the first analysis was written up, gave the same answer (R = 1.10). The few unusually divergent genes cluster in two chromosome regions, but on inspection those are places where the two diploids happen to share a nearly identical stretch of DNA, not places where the tetraploid is unusual. The two tetraploid pseudo-haplotypes differ from each other less than the diploid's own two haplotypes do. A power calculation shows the data rule out a second genome diverged by 0.5 % or more if it makes up a quarter or more of the deposited genes, and by 2 % or more if it makes up a tenth — against a 9 % gap to the next nettle species. Within the deposited sequence, then, the tetraploid is a doubled version of the ordinary Eurasian diploid, not a hybrid of two distinct lineages.

**The honest caveat.** The Darwin Tree of Life pseudo-haplotypes contain about two-thirds of the tetraploid's DNA "words" (k-mers). A divergent second genome that was discarded before deposition would be invisible to our test. The decisive check — a k-mer analysis of the raw reads, which is cheap for anyone with ~60 GB of disk and a day of compute — is specified in VALIDATION.md; it did not fit within this study's resource limits.

**Three further results.** (1) The German "diploid" nettle genome contains three or more near-identical copies of 24 % of genes, about twice what a diploid with both haplotypes pooled shows and three-quarters of the tetraploid value, on long contigs; that is halfway between what a diploid and what a tetraploid assembly show, and the depositors' own checks cannot tell the two apart — so that plant's ploidy is an open question they should settle with flow cytometry. (2) Genetic diversity is lowest in the middle of every nettle chromosome, and most deeply on the seven large chromosomes predicted to have "polycentric" centromeres — the opposite of our prediction, so the diversity landscape does not show the distributed recombination that true polycentricity would allow. (3) The chromosome that is split in two in *U. dioica* relative to the small nettle *U. urens* is an eight-piece mosaic, so "fission or fusion" cannot be decided with the outgroups available.

**Why it matters.** Autotetraploids and allotetraploids evolve differently: autotetraploids keep four interchangeable copies of every gene and inherit tetrasomically, which shapes how they adapt, how they can be bred, and how their genomes should be assembled. Anyone sequencing a nettle should expect a tetraploid of this kind — and should check ploidy before calling an assembly diploid.

**Everything is reproducible.** All inputs are public; the code (Snakemake workflow, BLAST+ 2.17, Python), the preregistration with its hash, the laboratory notebook, the reviewers' reports and every result table are provided. Total compute ≈ 30 h on a 4-core laptop; peak disk 14 GB.
