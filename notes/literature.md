# Urtica dioica genomics: literature map

Query date: 2026-10-22. Sources queried: OpenAlex (API key), CrossRef, Europe PMC (articles and preprints), Semantic Scholar, bioRxiv API, NCBI Datasets v2 and E-utilities (metadata only). Full texts read: Hirabayashi 2025 (Plants), Hirabayashi 2026 (Sci Data), Christenhusz 2025 (Wellcome Open Res), Rejlová 2019 (PLoS One), Cronk 2016 (BDJ), Xu 2019 (Plant Direct), Ma 2023 (Hortic Res). All other entries rest on abstracts and metadata, and are labelled as such. The companion files are `references.csv` (one row per cited work with topic tags and verification status) and `notes_constraints.md` (analyst rules derived from this map).

Scope decisions: the map covers *Urtica dioica* sensu lato and the Urticaceae comparators that bear on comparative analyses; pharmacology, food-science and extraction papers (the bulk of the ~800 Europe PMC hits) were excluded. Where the task named a resource that could not be found, the gap is stated under the topic rather than filled from recall.

## Genome resources

### 1. Genome assemblies and transcriptomes

Three independent chromosome-level assemblies of *U. dioica* exist, and they do not sample the same cytotype. The first published reference is a haplotype-resolved assembly of a diploid (2n = 26) female, clone 11-4, collected beside the River Jiu near Rovinari, Romania, built from PacBio HiFi, ONT, Illumina and Hi-C data; the two haplotypes span 574.9 Mb (H1; contig N50 10.9 Mb, scaffold N50 44.0 Mb) and 521.2 Mb (H2; 13.5 / 48.0 Mb) with BUSCO 92.6 % and 92.2 %, carry 20,333 and 20,140 annotated genes, and are 69.14 % / 68.59 % transposable element (LTR retrotransposons 47–51 % of chromosome length, TIRs 13–16 %; LAI 16.96 and 11.15) ([Hirabayashi 2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/plants14010124), open access; preprint [Hirabayashi 2024](https://doi.org/10.1101/2024.11.21.624787)). NCBI accessions are GCA_048565285.1 (H1, PRJNA663211) and GCA_048565295.1 (H2, PRJNA1198346), BioSample SAMN16122873 (NCBI Datasets, 2026-10-22). Heterozygosity estimated independently from short reads was ~1.5 %, and SyRI classified only ~71 % of the inter-haplotype alignment as syntenic with ~16 % unaligned, which the authors attribute to repeat content rather than to assembly error.

The second assembly is the male offspring of clone 11-4 (plant U48, from a cross 11-4 female × 7-5 male, the latter from the River Struma north of Boboshevo, Bulgaria), assembled from ~129× HiFi plus Hi-C with hifiasm, k-mer heterozygosity 1.85 %; MH1 and MH2 total 557,148,949 bp and 533,955,990 bp (scaffold N50 45.3 and 49.8 Mb), BUSCO C ≈ 93 %, D ≤ 3 % ([Hirabayashi 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41597-026-06960-7), open access; preprint [Hirabayashi 2025](https://doi.org/10.1101/2025.09.12.675976)). Because the male is a known offspring of the female reference, the authors assigned each male chromosome to a maternal or paternal origin by >99.7 % identity alignment to FH1/FH2 (their Table 2). Accessions: GCA_054948195.1 (MH1, PRJNA1308592), GCA_054948205.1 (MH2, PRJNA1308630; this haplotype also carries a RefSeq annotation GCF_054948205.1 under PRJNA1535076), SRA SRR35053641 (HiFi) and SRR35053640 (Hi-C), BioSample SAMN50702769; supplementary tables, annotations and TE annotations are on Figshare project 230981. The paper states that this assembly "confirms three previously reported nettle genome features": inter-haplotype structural variation including large inversions, likely polycentric centromeres, and the urticaceous pain-peptide genes.

The third is the Darwin Tree of Life assembly drUrtDioi1.1, GCA_964188135.1 (alternate haplotype GCA_964188125.1; umbrella BioProject PRJEB58083; data PRJEB58082; specimen KDTOL10015 from Queen Charlotte's Cottage, RBG Kew, collected 2020-08-05, voucher K001400687), 618.00 Mb in 3,280 scaffolds, contig N50 2.1 Mb, scaffold N50 37.3 Mb, QV 60.6, BUSCO C 91.4 % [S 76.4 %, D 15.0 %], k-mer completeness 55 % primary / 68 % combined, 79.4 % of the assembly in 13 pseudomolecules ([Christenhusz 2025](https://doi.org/10.12688/wellcomeopenres.23399.1), open access). The note's own abstract states that the 13 pseudomolecules represent "one subgenome of the tetraploid genome", that the genome was "curated in multiple rounds, initially on all four haplotypes and subsequently on a pseudo-primary haplotype 1", and the open peer review attached to the note attributes the low k-mer completeness and 15 % BUSCO duplication to tetraploidy. A plastid genome of 146.67 kb and a mitochondrial assembly in 12 sequences were deposited with it. The genome-size value measured by flow cytometry for this specimen is not printed in the note (it is held at links.tol.sanger.ac.uk/species/3501, which was not reachable from the sandbox).

A fourth assembly appeared as a preprint after the task's reference list was drawn up: a nanopore-based assembly of 1.1 Gb (N50 40.7 Mb) with RNA-seq-supported gene annotation focused on flavonoid and anthocyanin biosynthesis under high light and nitrogen deprivation ([Wolff 2026](https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.05.15.725508), bioRxiv, University of Bonn; abstract only — the PDF text layer could not be parsed in the sandbox and the JATS endpoint rate-limited). The corresponding raw data are very probably ENA project PRJEB63450 ("Nanopore sequencing of Urtica dioica", IZMB Bonn, MinION R9.4.1, registered 2024-12-31). The 1.1 Gb span is roughly twice the monoploid genome and is consistent either with an uncollapsed diploid or with a partially collapsed tetraploid; the ploidy of the sequenced plant is not stated in the abstract and must be treated as unknown. This assembly is not in NCBI Datasets under *Urtica* as of the query date.

Related Urticaceae references used for synteny are *U. urens* drUrtUren1.1, GCA_958296335.1, 339.6 Mb in 12 pseudomolecules, 18,378 Ensembl protein-coding genes, with 335.02 kb mitochondrial and 147.51 kb plastid genomes ([Christenhusz 2024](https://doi.org/10.12688/wellcomeopenres.23187.1), open access), *Parietaria judaica* ([Christenhusz 2024](https://doi.org/10.12688/wellcomeopenres.23153.1), open access), and the ramie genomes (Topic 5). No earlier draft or short-read *U. dioica* nuclear assembly was found in NCBI, ENA metadata or the literature; the DToL assembly is the only tetraploid-derived one.

Transcriptome resources predate the genomes. The fibre "clone 13" (selected by Bredemann 1927–1950) was sequenced by Illumina MiSeq/NextSeq across internodes, core and cortical tissues, leaves, roots and calli; de novo assembly gave 36,320 contigs (N50 1,150 bp), and the data are under PRJNA518071 ([Xu 2019](https://doi.org/10.1002/pld3.151), open access). The same group produced the callose-synthase family inventory ([Guerriero 2020](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijms21113853), open access) and a callogenesis transcriptome under PRJNA767836 ([Xu 2021](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijms222212319), open access); a mechanical-stress expression study followed ([Zajączkowska 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00425-024-04477-0)). NCBI SRA lists 98 RNA-seq runs for *U. dioica* (2026-10-22), including DToL RNA-seq (SAMEA7521894) and a 1KP transcriptome (PRJEB21674). The DToL note and all three Hirabayashi/Christenhusz papers are gold open access; the Bonn preprint is green.

**Open questions / gaps (Topic 1).** (i) No publication yet compares all four *U. dioica* assemblies, and no assembly represents the tetraploid with both subgenomes resolved. (ii) The ploidy of the Wolff 2026 individual is unverified. (iii) No pangenome or population-scale resequencing of *U. dioica* exists. (iv) The RefSeq annotation covers only the male H2 haplotype; the female haplotypes and the DToL assembly have no NCBI annotation (DToL annotation, if any, is on Ensembl Plants, not checked).

### 2. Cytotype, subspecies and genome size

*U. dioica* is a mixed-ploidy complex in which the tetraploid is numerically dominant and the diploids are taxonomically and ecologically distinct. In the largest survey, 7,012 plants from 1,317 populations were ploidy-typed by flow cytometry: 87 % tetraploid, 13 % diploid, with 8 triploids and 4 pentaploids found in one mixed population; chromosome counts on ten plants gave 2n = 26 for diploids (plants assigned to subsp. *pubescens*, *sondenii* and *subinermis*), 2n = 39 (triploid), 2n = 52 (five tetraploid subsp. *dioica*) and 2n = 65 ([Rejlová 2019](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0218389), open access). The same paper reports a uniform monoploid genome size (Cx ≈ 0.55–0.59 pg) across core *U. dioica* except subsp. *cypria*, 2C of 1.10–1.21 pg for diploids and 2.08–2.20 pg for tetraploids, eight tetraploid outliers at 2C 1.66–1.74 pg that the authors suggest may correspond to the repeatedly reported 2n = 48 count, and seed-ploidy screening of a mixed Moravian population indicating that inter-cytotype gene flow occurs. Diploids were associated with natural and near-natural habitats (alluvial and ravine forests, alpine vegetation, tundra mires) and tetraploids with synanthropic sites; a follow-up niche analysis concluded that whole-genome duplication broadened the ecological niche ([Urfus 2021](https://doi.org/10.23855/preslia.2021.305); abstract not retrievable, title and citing context only).

An independent, smaller transect gave the genome-size numbers most often quoted for the diploid: among 29 willow-associated plants from Greece to arctic Norway, 5 were diploid (two in Bulgaria and Romania, three in southern Finland) with 2C 1.31–1.35 pg, "equivalent to a haploid genome size of c. 650 Mbp", and 24 tetraploid with 2C 2.36–2.59 pg, the most northerly tetraploids being largest ([Cronk 2016](https://doi.org/10.3897/bdj.4.e10003), open access). The ~15 % discrepancy between the Rejlová and Cronk absolute values (0.55–0.60 vs ~0.66 pg per Cx) is a calibration difference between laboratories, not a biological signal; both agree that tetraploid 2C is almost exactly twice diploid 2C. Cronk and colleagues also note that both 2n = 48 and 2n = 52 have been reported repeatedly for tetraploids (citing Skalińska 1974) and "the repeated reports of both numbers leads to a suspicion that both numbers do exist in nature." Hirabayashi 2025 summarises the species as "either diploid (2n = 24, 26) or tetraploid (2n = 48, 52)" with diploid genome-size estimates ranging 558–660 Mb; their own clone 11-4 is 2n = 26 and the female assembly is 521–575 Mb per haplotype. The Plant DNA C-values database itself (cvalues.science.kew.org) was not reachable from the sandbox and was not queried; the primary flow-cytometry values above are the ones that feed it.

Taxonomically, the diploids are the named Eurasian subspecies (*subinermis*, *sondenii*, *pubescens*, *kurdistanica*, plus the Cyprus endemic *cypria* described by [Weigend 2006](https://doi.org/10.3372/wi.36.36212)), and the tetraploid is subsp. *dioica*; this ploidy-based delimitation follows [Grosse-Veldmann 2015](https://doi.org/10.11646/phytotaxa.208.4.1) ("Weeding the nettles III"). The North American plants formerly called *U. dioica* subsp. *gracilis* were rehabilitated as the species *U. gracilis* on morphological and molecular grounds ([Henning 2014](https://doi.org/10.11646/phytotaxa.162.2.1)); *U. gracilis* is monoecious whereas *U. dioica* is dioecious, as Hirabayashi 2025 stresses, and its chromosome numbers were treated in the classic North American biosystematic series ([Woodland 1982](https://doi.org/10.2307/2418389); title and DOI verified, text not retrieved). A genus-wide phylogeny (61 of 63 species; ITS, psbA-trnH, trnL-F, trnS-trnG) places the *U. dioica* group within a well-resolved tree but leaves infraspecific relationships unresolved ([Grosse-Veldmann 2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ympev.2016.05.019)). Hyb-Seq of 279 plants spanning Europe and south-west Asia found that diploid subspecies separate morphologically "but our molecular evaluation did not reveal any structure", and that tetraploids "coalesced with diploids in both morphological and molecular analyses" ([Rejlová 2020](https://doi.org/10.1093/botlinnean/boaa076), abstract via Semantic Scholar; raw reads PRJNA602985, Institute of Botany, Czech Academy of Sciences). The British ecological synthesis remains the best single entry to older cytological and ecological literature ([Taylor 2009](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2745.2009.01575.x)).

On the origin of the tetraploid, Rejlová 2019 writes that it "is also often supposed to be an allopolyploid or a group of allopolyploids with different evolutionary histories", citing Geltman's Russian-language work; no genomic test of auto- versus allotetraploidy has been published. The Hyb-Seq result that tetraploids are not genetically separable from diploids argues against a distant second parent, but the probe set was not designed to phase subgenomes.

**Open questions / gaps (Topic 2).** (i) Auto- vs allotetraploid origin is untested with genomic data; the DToL "one subgenome" assembly is the only tetraploid sequence and its subgenomes are not separated. (ii) Whether 2n = 48 tetraploids are real dysploids or aneuploids is unresolved (Rejlová could not grow the outlier plants). (iii) Geographic distribution of diploids outside Europe/SW Asia (Asia, N. America) is not surveyed by flow cytometry in any retrieved paper. (iv) No published Plant DNA C-values entry was checked directly.

### 3. The chromosome 8 inversion

The two haplotypes of the female reference "differed by a massive inversion (18.4 Mbp in length) on chromosome 8, which was surrounded by multiple duplicated regions"; chromosomes 1, 2, 3 and 8 carried the most structural variation ([Hirabayashi 2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/plants14010124), their Figures 2–3 and S3). Validation was threefold and is stated explicitly: "We manually checked all those SVs using Hi-C data and looked for HiFi and Nanopore reads spanning their breakpoints; this included manually 'correcting' these SVs in either haplotype to determine if this resulted in improved Hi-C contact maps. In all cases, these checks supported the current chromosome organization of the two haplotypes" (Tables S3 and S4 record rounds 3–4 of Hi-C curation and the long-read breakpoint checks). The authors additionally re-scaffolded their contigs onto the DToL primary haplotype with RagTag and re-mapped Hi-C to show that differences from Udio_DToL did not behave as misassemblies. They link the instability of chromosome 8 to its fusion/fission history relative to *U. urens* (Topic 5).

The male assembly extends rather than contradicts this: chromosome 8 "stands out for its multiple large, nested inversions and high levels of repetitive sequences", the region "displays multiple multi-Mbp chromosomal inversions and other rearrangements, including a possible 8 Mbp insertion in the paternally-inherited haplotype that is not observed in the maternal haplotypes" (their Fig. 5b), and a large repeat-rich contig interacting only with the centre of chromosome 8 of MH2 was manually inserted into that chromosome during Hi-C curation ([Hirabayashi 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41597-026-06960-7)). Structural variants were concentrated on chromosomes 1, 2, 3, 6 and 8 in the male. Because parental origin is known for each male haplotype, the inversion's segregation can in principle be traced between the two generations; the paper does not report which female haplotype orientation the male inherited. No population-level genotyping of the inversion, no recombination-suppression estimate and no breakpoint-gene analysis has been published.

**Open questions / gaps (Topic 3).** (i) Frequency and geographic distribution of the two chromosome 8 arrangements in natural populations are unknown. (ii) Whether the inversion is polymorphic within tetraploids, and how it maps onto the DToL pseudomolecule, is not reported. (iii) Breakpoint content and the nature of the flanking duplications (segmental vs TE-mediated) are undescribed. (iv) No evidence yet distinguishes an adaptive-inversion from a sex-linked-inversion interpretation (Topic 6).

### 4. Centromere architecture

The polycentric/acrocentric classification rests entirely on tandem-repeat pattern analysis of the assemblies, not on CENH3 or any cytological evidence. The method is RepeatOBserver, a Fourier-transform DNA-walk tool whose authors report that 93 % of experimentally verified tandem-repeat centromeres fall in regions of low repeat Shannon diversity and 97 % of retrotransposon centromeres in regions of high repeat-length abundance across 159 chromosomes from 12 species ([Elphinstone 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/1755-0998.14084), open access). Applied to the female genome, "repeat patterns are consistent with the presence of acrocentric or near telocentric centromeres in five out of 13 chromosomes (chromosomes 8, 9, 11, 12, 13). For eight more chromosomes (chromosomes 1–7, 10) we observed more diffused and fragmented patterns of repeats over a large proportion of the chromosome, suggesting the presence of polycentric centromere"; the predicted positions coincide with gene-density minima (Table S8), and the authors caution that "these analyses only provide a putative centromere location, and that definitive identification" requires further work ([Hirabayashi 2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/plants14010124)). The male paper reports that "the same putative centromere type (polycentric, metacentric, acrocentric) was predicted for the different chromosomes between male and female assemblies" ([Hirabayashi 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41597-026-06960-7)); note that this is the first time chromosome 10 is singled out as metacentric rather than grouped with 1–7, and the two papers' wording differs (1–7 and 10 "polycentric" in 2025; "polycentric, metacentric, acrocentric" as three classes in 2026). The task's framing — chromosomes 1–7 polycentric, 10 metacentric, 8, 9, 11, 12, 13 acrocentric — corresponds to the 2026 wording. Both papers state that polycentric centromeres "had not been previously reported in the Urticaceae family, but is known in the closely related Moraceae", and that ramie shows acrocentric centromeres.

The Moraceae precedent is the only Rosales case with direct evidence: in the gap-free *Morus notabilis* genome, CENH3 ChIP-seq domains co-localise with arrays of a conserved satellite (m3cp) at multiple sites per chromosome, chromosome 3 for instance carrying two centromere units each of two CENH3 domains within 7 Mb, and the authors describe the species as "the only reported polycentric chromosome species up to now" and propose a fusion–fission model ([Ma 2023](https://doi.org/10.1093/hr/uhad111), open access). The broader literature on multi-domain monocentrics is the legume metapolycentromere: pea and *Lathyrus* chromosomes carry 3–5 separate CENH3 domains along an extended primary constriction ([Neumann 2012](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pgen.1002777), open access), with distinct histone marks ([Neumann 2016](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpls.2016.00234)), and the 81.6 Mb pea CEN6 assembly showed genes, methylation and most repeats uniformly distributed across the centromere with satellite arrays up to 2 Mb ([Macas 2023](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pgen.1010633), open access; review [Grishko 2024](https://doi.org/10.18699/vjgb-24-66)). No satellite family, no CENH3 sequence, no ChIP, no immunostaining and no Hi-C compartment analysis has been reported for *U. dioica* centromeres.

**Open questions / gaps (Topic 4).** (i) Zero functional evidence: the centromere classes are repeat-pattern predictions. (ii) The identity of the *U. dioica* centromeric satellite(s) and whether the same family occupies "polycentric" and "acrocentric" chromosomes is unknown. (iii) Whether the diffuse-repeat chromosomes are true polycentrics (several CENH3 domains), metapolycentrics, or monocentrics with dispersed pericentromeric satellites cannot be decided from the published data. (iv) The chromosome 10 "metacentric" call appears only in the 2026 paper's summary sentence.

## Chromosome and sex evolution

### 5. Karyotype evolution in Urticaceae and Rosales

Base chromosome number varies within *Urtica* and the fusion/fission between *U. dioica* and *U. urens* is now mapped. "Base chromosome number in U. urens and many other related Urtica species is often n = 12, whereas U. dioica is almost always n = 13 in diploid cytotypes, while n = 12 is more frequently observed in tetraploids", and "U. dioica chromosomes 8 and 13 are syntenic with chromosome 2 in U. urens, highlighting a clear history of chromosome fusion/fission" ([Hirabayashi 2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/plants14010124), Figure 5; the n = 12 counts are drawn from [Sharma 1979](https://doi.org/10.1508/cytologia.44.799) and [de Lange 2002](https://doi.org/10.1080/0028825X.2002.9512767)). The DToL *U. urens* assembly has 12 pseudomolecules at 339.6 Mb ([Christenhusz 2024](https://doi.org/10.12688/wellcomeopenres.23187.1)), i.e. half the monoploid size of *U. dioica* with one fewer chromosome — a point relevant to any TE-expansion comparison. Synteny across *U. dioica*, *U. urens*, *Boehmeria nivea* and *Parietaria judaica* was "quite conserved" despite rearrangements, and higher between *Urtica* and *Boehmeria* than between either and *Parietaria* although the phylogenetic distances are similar (Hirabayashi 2025). The family backbone used for such comparisons is now the 345-species plastome + nrDNA phylogeny that divides Urticaceae into seven tribes, sinks *Hesperocnide* into *Urtica*, and reports plastid–nuclear incongruence at several nodes ([Fu 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pld.2025.12.003)), superseding the earlier multi-locus tree ([Wu 2013](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ympev.2013.06.022)); dated biogeography is in [Wu 2018](https://doi.org/10.1111/ele.13132). A family-wide chromosome-number compilation (the task's "7–14" range) was not retrieved: neither CCDB nor a Urticaceae karyotype review surfaced in any index, and the only genus-level numbers verified here are *Urtica* n = 12/13 and *Morus* n = 7/14.

Within Rosales, the best-documented karyotype transitions are in Moraceae and Cannabaceae. *Morus notabilis* (n = 7, later shown to be n = 6 in the chromosome-scale assembly) and *M. alba* (n = 14) are related by fusion/fission with high synteny verified by FISH, LTR retrotransposons driving divergence ([Xuan 2022](https://doi.org/10.1093/hr/uhab030), open access); an independent analysis reconstructed a ~14-protochromosome ancestor and descending dysploidy to 2n = 12 by fusions, and mapped a ~6.2 Mb sex-determining region on chromosome 3 ([Xia 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.gpb.2022.08.005), open access; draft genome [He 2013](https://doi.org/10.1038/ncomms3445)). The polycentric interpretation of *Morus* (Topic 4) is the proposed mechanism allowing fused chromosomes to persist. For Cannabaceae, the physical/genetic map of *Cannabis sativa* identified extensive rearrangements ([Laverty 2018](https://doi.org/10.1101/gr.242594.118)), and the 2025 pangenome gives a population-scale view of structural variation ([Lynch 2025](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-09065-0)). Ramie provides the within-Urticaceae comparator with the most genomic depth: a draft genome (335.6 Mb, 42,463 genes; [Liu 2017](https://doi.org/10.1093/dnares/dsx047)), two chromosome-level wild and cultivated assemblies plus a resequencing-based domestication scan ([Wang 2021](https://doi.org/10.1111/tpj.15346)), and a feral-ramie assembly with 915 resequenced accessions ([Wu 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.xplc.2024.100942)). The general framework for interpreting dysploidy after polyploidy is [Mandáková 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pbi.2018.03.001). No published synteny analysis places *U. dioica* chromosomes against *Cannabis* or *Morus* explicitly; Hirabayashi 2025 compared only within Urticaceae.

**Open questions / gaps (Topic 5).** (i) Direction of the chr8/chr13 event (fusion in *U. urens* lineage vs fission in *U. dioica*) is not resolved; an outgroup synteny analysis (ramie, *Parietaria*, *Morus*) is needed. (ii) The n = 12 tetraploid counts may indicate an independent fusion in the tetraploid lineage; no tetraploid karyotype has been sequenced to test this. (iii) No ancestral Rosales/Urticaceae karyotype reconstruction including *Urtica* exists. (iv) Family-wide chromosome-number data were not verified here.

### 6. Sex determination

Male heterogamety in *U. dioica* is an old inference from a single kind of experiment, and the sex-determining region is unmapped. Hirabayashi 2026 summarises the primary evidence: "Evidence for male heterogamety was first obtained by Strasburger, who selfed a female plant that had formed some aberrant individual male flowers, obtaining only female progeny" (Strasburger 1910; Correns 1928), and states that the male "is believed to be the heterogametic sex". Crossing work in the 2000s found the genetics to be more complex than a single XY locus. Shannon and Holsinger crossed dioecious and monoecious subspecies and forms and concluded that "most significant is evidence for a maternal influence on sex determination and for the possibility of gynodioecy as an intermediate stage" ([Shannon 2006](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00497-006-0041-5)). De Jong and Glawe showed that seed sex ratio (fraction of males) in a Dutch dune population varied between 0.05 and 0.76 among maternal families and responded to one generation of selection to 0.08 and 0.73 ([de Jong 2005](https://doi.org/10.1055/s-2005-837540)); reciprocal crosses then showed that "maternal parents strongly contribute to the variation in the primary sex ratio" and that progeny sex ratios "resembled the sex ratios produced by their maternal parents" ([Glawe 2006](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1420-9101.2006.01215.x)); the synthesis is titled "complex sex determination" ([Glawe 2008](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10682-008-9261-5); abstract not retrievable, summarised in Hirabayashi 2026 as "complex inheritance patterns and significant maternal effects on seed sex ratios in dioecious Urtica, while monoecious individuals produced varying numbers of female flowers based on environmental conditions, suggesting a wholly genetic basis for sex determination in dioecious types but environmental influences in monoecious types"). The task's phrasing "one major locus + modifiers + maternal effect" is a fair gloss of these three papers but no paper states a locus count. Monoecy is common even within a dioecious population: in a Russian population, true monoecy occurred in 10 of 36 genets and flowers with sexual abnormalities co-occurred on single ramets, interpreted as paradioecious lability ([Popova 2024](https://doi.org/10.1111/1442-1984.12486)).

No SDR has been mapped and no sex-biased expression study of *U. dioica* exists in any index queried. The male genome paper is explicit that the SDR is its next step: the male assembly "represents a first step towards elucidating the control of sex determination", "will be foundational to further studies aimed at characterizing the sex-determining region (SDR)", and "a highly variable region in chromosome 8 presents several of the hallmarks of an SDR, including complex large inversions and a possible 8 Mbp insertion in the paternally-inherited haplotype, and will be a promising candidate" ([Hirabayashi 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41597-026-06960-7)). This is a hypothesis stated by the data-generating group, not a result; chromosome 8 is also the chromosome with the fusion/fission history (Topic 5), so repeat accumulation and inversions there have at least one non-sexual explanation. The nearest resolved systems are in sister families: Cannabaceae have heteromorphic XY shared by hop and cannabis for ~36 Myr with a degenerated male-specific Y, where an X-linked ACS homolog, not a Y gene, determines female inflorescence fate ([Carey 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-026-73233-7)); a haplotype-resolved hemp genome quantifies X–Y divergence ([Wei 2025](https://doi.org/10.1093/hr/uhaf268)); and *Morus notabilis* carries a ~6.2 Mb SDR on chromosome 3 with a partially duplicated DNA-helicase and three Gypsy elements as male-specific candidates ([Xia 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.gpb.2022.08.005)).

**Open questions / gaps (Topic 6).** (i) SDR location, size and age are unknown; the chromosome 8 candidacy is unverified (flagged next step of the UBC group). (ii) No test of male heterogamety by sex-linked markers or k-mer sex association exists. (iii) Monoecious *U. gracilis* vs dioecious *U. dioica* offers a comparative design nobody has used genomically. (iv) The maternal effect on sex ratio has no mechanistic candidate (cytoplasmic? imprinting?).

## Trait biology

### 7. Stinging-hair toxins

The peptide toxins of Urticeae are now characterised biochemically, and *U. dioica* carries a duplicated urthionin gene. The older small-molecule account (histamine, acetylcholine, serotonin; oxalic and tartaric acid as persistent pain agents in *U. thunbergiana*, [Fu 2006](https://doi.org/10.1093/aob/mcl089); trichome morphology reviewed by [Thurston 1969](https://doi.org/10.1007/bf02858878)) was superseded in 2020 when *Dendrocnide* stinging-hair venom was shown to contain 4 kDa inhibitory-cystine-knot "gympietides" that delay NaV inactivation, "an intriguing example of inter-kingdom convergent evolution of animal and plant venoms" ([Gilding 2020](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.abb8828), open access). Their pharmacology requires TMEM233: excelsatoxin A activity at NaV1.7 depends on this dispanin accessory protein ([Jami 2023](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-023-37963-2), open access). For *Urtica*, the New Zealand tree nettle yields two classes: Δ-Uf1a (urthionin), a 42-residue cytotoxic thionin that disrupts membranes, and β/δ-Uf2a (urticatoxin), a 63-residue NaV modulator (EC50 0.86 nM at NaV1.6), and "both toxin classes are widespread among members of the Urticeae tribe", with the authors stating that *Urtica* and *Dendrocnide* peptides are structurally distinct arsenals ([Xie 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbc.2022.102218), open access).

Genomically, Hirabayashi 2025 "identified two copies of a gene putatively encoding the urthionin peptide on chromosome 9 (86% amino acid match to the U. ferox mature peptide: Δ-Uf1a). The two paralogs were positioned right next to each other with the same sequences, indicating a recent gene duplication event. Hits for urticatoxin on chromosomes 6 and 9 were only a 26–49% match … suggesting that this class of peptides might not be found in U. dioica. No genes with similarity to … Excelsatoxin A and Moroidotoxin A were identified." The male genome found the same two urthionin A copies on chromosome 9 in both haplotypes ([Hirabayashi 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41597-026-06960-7), Supplementary Table 2). Convergence is therefore asserted at two levels: gympietides vs animal knottins (Gilding 2020), and urticatoxins vs gympietides within Urticaceae (Xie 2022, repeated by Hirabayashi 2025). The older nettle protein literature concerns the hevein-domain lectin UDA, whose gene encodes a lectin plus chitinase domain ([Lerner 1992](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0021-9258%2818%2941729-2)) with isolectin structure from [Beintema 1992](https://doi.org/10.1016/0014-5793%2892%2980231-5); no genome-era analysis of the UDA family has been published. No *Dendrocnide* genome or trichome transcriptome exists in any index queried.

**Open questions / gaps (Topic 7).** (i) Whether the two chromosome-9 urthionin copies are expressed in trichomes and whether copy number varies across cytotypes/populations is untested. (ii) The absence of a urticatoxin orthologue in *U. dioica* is inferred from low identity hits only; a tribe-wide gene-family phylogeny of thionin-like and urticatoxin-like genes has not been done. (iii) Gympietide genes have never been located in a genome. (iv) UDA/hevein gene-family evolution in the genome is unexplored.

### 8. Bast-fibre genes

Fibre biology in nettle has transcriptomic but not genomic coverage. The clone-13 transcriptome localised secondary-cell-wall programmes to the stem core (CesA4/IRX5, CesA7/IRX3, CesA8/IRX1; hemicellulose and lignin genes), detected a carbon-starvation signal in cortical tissue during fibre thickening, and documented a lamellar gelatinous layer and galactose-rich rhamnogalacturonan in mature bast fibres ([Xu 2019](https://doi.org/10.1002/pld3.151)); the same group profiled callose synthases ([Guerriero 2020](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijms21113853)), and the agronomic/varietal background including the Bredemann clones is reviewed by [Viotti 2022](https://doi.org/10.3390/ma15124288). In ramie, the draft genome reported five stem-bark-specific cellulose synthase genes and WAT1-related genes among lineage-unique families ([Liu 2017](https://doi.org/10.1093/dnares/dsx047)), a bark transcriptome ranked fibre-development genes ([Xie 2020](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12864-020-6457-8)), domestication sweeps enriched for fibre-growth genes were mapped from two assemblies plus resequencing ([Wang 2021](https://doi.org/10.1111/tpj.15346)) and from 301 accessions ([Zeng 2021](https://doi.org/10.1111/pbi.13714)), with a reference transcriptome in [Al-Ani 2018](https://doi.org/10.3390/fib6010008). For hemp, the genome and transcriptome of [van Bakel 2011](https://doi.org/10.1186/gb-2011-12-10-r102) and the improved assembly of [Grassa 2021](https://doi.org/10.1111/nph.17243) are the standard comparators. No paper has yet compared fibre gene families (CesA, COBRA, FLA, LAC, CAD) across nettle, ramie, hemp and flax at the whole-genome level, and the *U. dioica* genome papers do not analyse fibre genes.

**Open questions / gaps (Topic 8).** (i) No genome-anchored fibre gene-family inventory for nettle. (ii) The genetic basis of the ~3-fold fibre-yield difference between Bredemann clones and wild plants is unknown; clone 13 has not been sequenced. (iii) Convergent fibre-crop evolution across Rosales (Urticaceae, Cannabaceae) and Malpighiales (flax) is an open comparative question.

### 9. Nitrophily

The ecological evidence for nitrate/phosphate tracking is classical and strong; the molecular basis is entirely unstudied. Olsen established the association with nutrient-rich soils ([Olsen 1921](https://doi.org/10.2307/2255757)), and Pigott and Taylor showed that nettle distribution in woodland tracks soil phosphate as much as nitrogen ([Pigott 1964](https://doi.org/10.2307/2438)); nitrate versus ammonium nutrition effects on growth and N partitioning were measured by [Rosnitschek-Schimmel 1982](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0044-328x%2882%2980179-7); genecological differentiation of populations is in [Pollard 1984](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1469-8137.1984.tb03615.x); fertiliser responses and tissue element concentrations (Ca, N, P accumulation) in a cut grassland are in [Müllerová 2014](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.actao.2014.05.004); the synthesis is [Taylor 2009](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2745.2009.01575.x). The only genomic entry points are indirect: the Bonn preprint frames anthocyanin genes as nitrogen-deprivation responders ([Wolff 2026](https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.05.15.725508), abstract), the ramie draft genome reported 22 of 106 positively selected genes in nitrogen metabolism ([Liu 2017](https://doi.org/10.1093/dnares/dsx047)), and the NPF (NRT1/PTR) family has been inventoried in hemp (66 genes; [Yue 2026](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-08990-0)) but not in any Urticaceae. No nitrate transporter, nitrate reductase or GS/GOGAT gene-family study exists for nettle.

**Open questions / gaps (Topic 9).** (i) NPF/NRT2/AMT/NR gene-family sizes and expression in nettle vs non-nitrophilous relatives are unknown. (ii) Whether nitrophily is a tetraploid trait (synanthropic habitats; Rejlová 2019) or shared by diploids is untested. (iii) No root transcriptome under N treatments exists for *U. dioica*.

### 10. Transposable elements, organellar genomes, orphan genes, horizontal transfer

TE content is quantified only in the two UBC assemblies: 69.14 % / 68.59 % of H1/H2, LTR retrotransposons 47.23 % / 50.51 % and TIR elements 15.63 % / 13.24 % of chromosome length, LAI 16.96 / 11.15, TE density in 500 kb windows ranging 0 to >6,000 with 37 windows above 2,000 ([Hirabayashi 2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/plants14010124), Table S7); the male paper deposited its TE annotation on Figshare. No analysis of TE age distributions, family-level dynamics, or TE differences between cytotypes has been published; the *U. urens* genome at ~340 Mb with 12 chromosomes implies a roughly two-fold repeat difference between the two species that nobody has dissected.

Organellar genomes are abundant but unanalysed for *U. dioica* itself. NCBI holds complete plastomes for *U. dioica* (NC_064948.1, 147,799 bp; OM761947.1, 146,928 bp), *U. dioica* subsp. *xingjiangensis* (NC_062305.1), *U. angustifolia*, *U. urens* (NC_064958.1; PV008708.1 from the DToL specimen), *U. cannabina* (PQ846489.1) and ~20 further *Urtica* species, almost all from the Urticeae genome-skimming study that found tribe plastomes of 145–161 kb with 110–112 genes, IR expansions/contractions and inversions ([Ogoma 2022](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpls.2022.870949), open access). Family-wide plastome comparisons report weak codon bias and 97 % of genes under purifying selection ([Gao 2026](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpls.2026.1846589)), and plastome structural variation congruent with *Boehmeria* polyphyly ([Zhan 2024](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpls.2024.1297499)). The DToL note assembled a 146.67 kb plastome and 12 mitochondrial sequences for *U. dioica*, and *U. urens* has a 335.02 kb mitogenome ([Christenhusz 2024](https://doi.org/10.12688/wellcomeopenres.23187.1)); no *U. dioica* mitogenome paper exists (E-utilities: 0 records for "mitochondrion complete"). For context on what mitogenome analyses can reveal in a holocentric comparator, Rhynchospora mitogenomes are giant because of nuclear-gene and retrotransposon insertions ([Almeida 2025](https://doi.org/10.1093/aob/mcaf098)).

Orphan genes and horizontal gene transfer: no study of lineage-specific genes in *Urtica* or Urticaceae was found, and no HGT report involving Urticaceae was found in OpenAlex or Europe PMC (the only HGT hits were parasitic plants, which are not Urticaceae). These are true absences in the retrieved literature, not failures of a named resource.

**Open questions / gaps (Topic 10).** (i) TE family dynamics, insertion ages and cytotype differences are unstudied. (ii) No *U. dioica* mitogenome has been annotated or published. (iii) Orphan-gene and HGT analyses do not exist for the family. (iv) Organellar–nuclear transfers (NUPTs/NUMTs) in the nettle genome are undescribed.

### 11. Population genetics and phylogeography

Population-level genetics of *U. dioica* is thin: one Hyb-Seq study, two cytogeographic surveys, and no SSR, RAD or whole-genome resequencing paper. The Hyb-Seq study of 279 plants (Europe and SW Asia) found no molecular structure among diploid subspecies and no separation of tetraploids from diploids, proposing recent diversification and homoploid/heteroploid hybridisation as explanations ([Rejlová 2020](https://doi.org/10.1093/botlinnean/boaa076); reads PRJNA602985). Cytogeography is covered by [Rejlová 2019](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0218389) (1,317 populations; relative genome size correlates positively with latitude and longitude in Europe) and [Cronk 2016](https://doi.org/10.3897/bdj.4.e10003) (diploids in Bulgaria/Romania and S. Finland; northern tetraploids larger). The genus phylogeny samples *U. dioica* s.l. accessions but with four Sanger loci ([Grosse-Veldmann 2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ympev.2016.05.019)), and the N. American taxa were split off as *U. gracilis* ([Henning 2014](https://doi.org/10.11646/phytotaxa.162.2.1)). The Urticaceae542 probe set was validated on herbarium specimens, making herbarium phylogenomics feasible, but has not yet been applied to a *U. dioica* population question ([Chien 2025](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-31584-z), open access). Ecological genetics of the nettle's specialist psyllid shows isolation-by-distance along the same transect ([Wonglersak 2017](https://doi.org/10.3897/bdj.5.e10824)). Genecological differentiation (stinging-hair density, growth) across British populations was shown by [Pollard 1984](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1469-8137.1984.tb03615.x). ENA genome-skim projects of Alpine (PRJEB82787) and Norwegian arctic floras (PRJEB43865) include *U. dioica* samples but have not been analysed for nettle. No herbarium-genomics study of *U. dioica* was found.

**Open questions / gaps (Topic 11).** (i) No genome-wide population sample of either cytotype exists. (ii) Origin(s) and number of tetraploid lineages are unknown. (iii) Asian and North American *U. dioica* s.s. (as opposed to *U. gracilis*) are unsampled genetically. (iv) Herbarium time series are untouched.

## Comparative framework

### 12. Polycentric and holocentric plant chromosomes

The holocentric plant literature has answered three questions that bear directly on a genome with mixed centromere types, and left others open. First, repeat-based holocentromeres are real and diverse: in *Rhynchospora*, CENH3 binds genome-wide arrays of the Tyba satellite ([Marques 2015](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1512255112)); chromosome-scale genomes of three *Rhynchospora* species versus monocentric *Juncus effusus* showed that holocentricity "affected 3D genome architecture by redefining genomic compartments", distributing function to thousands of units, and that "chromosome fusions, facilitated by repeat-based holocentromeres, promoted karyotype evolution and diploidization" ([Hofstatter 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2022.06.045)); *Chionographis japonica* has only 7–11 evenly spaced megabase-sized CENH3 units per chromatid with 23/28-bp palindromic satellites and interphase chromocentres like a monocentric ([Kuo 2023](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-023-38922-7), open access); *Luzula sylvatica* holocentromeres are associated with two satellites (Lusy1, Lusy2) but CENH3 also binds satellite-free gene-poor regions, and synteny with *Juncus* (n = 21 vs n = 6) implies origin by fusion of monocentric ancestors ([Mata-Sucre 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-024-53944-5), open access; cytology in [Heckmann 2011](https://doi.org/10.1159/000327713), meiosis in [Heckmann 2014](https://doi.org/10.1038/ncomms5979)); and in *Cuscuta* the transition dismantled the kinetochore (KNL2 lost; CENP-C, KNL1, ZWINT1 truncated; SAC degenerated) ([Neumann 2023](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2300877120), open access; chromosome-level *C. campestris* in [Cerda-Herrera 2025](https://doi.org/10.1093/g3journal/jkaf193)). Nutmeg holocentricity is not satellite-based ([Kuo 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10577-024-09751-1)). Reviews: [Mandrioli 2020](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pgen.1008918), [Kuo 2024](https://doi.org/10.1002/bies.202400013).

Second, the recombination landscape: in *R. breviuscula*, single-pollen sequencing showed crossovers "distally biased, in sharp contrast to the diffused distribution of hundreds of centromeric units", abolished inside centromere units but not near them, i.e. "the absence of a canonical centromere effect", attributed to telomere-led synapsis ([Castellani 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41477-024-01625-y)); sex-specific recombination maps exist for a holocentric species ([Chmielewski 2025](https://doi.org/10.1093/genetics/iyaf217)); and across 73 insects and 157 monocentric plants, monocentrics have nearly twice as many crossovers per chromosome as holocentrics, with mean chromosome size the best predictor of genome-wide rate, especially in holocentrics ([Zedek 2025](https://doi.org/10.1093/genetics/iyaf247)). Haplotype-specific chromosome painting of recombination ([Nascimento 2026](https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.06.24.733714), preprint), a holocentric pangenome linking karyotype evolution to recombination ([Zhang 2026](https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.01.17.700048), preprint), and pangenome dynamics of holocentromere repeats ([Włodzimierz 2026](https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.01.17.700053), preprint) extend this.

Third, karyotype evolution: holocentric *Carex* show conserved synteny despite high rearrangement rates because breakpoints cluster in genomic hotspots ([Escudero 2023](https://doi.org/10.1111/mec.17086)); oligo-painting traces fusion/fission in *Rhynchospora* ([Mata-Sucre 2024](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpls.2024.1330927)); polycentromeric drive is proposed to accelerate chromosomal evolution in sedges and rushes ([McCulloch 2026](https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.02.27.708619), preprint); hybridisation and polyploidy shaped *Luzula* radiations ([Heimer 2025](https://doi.org/10.1093/sysbio/syaf065)); and mobilome evolution in *Rhynchospora* is driven by repeat competition and ecological shifts rather than holocentromere abundance ([Costa 2024](https://doi.org/10.1093/aob/mcae220)). Meiotic adaptations are reviewed by [Hofstatter 2021](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpls.2021.658296).

What these systems have *not* answered, and where *U. dioica* is unusual: the holocentric and metapolycentric plants studied at genome scale are uniform across their karyotypes (all chromosomes holocentric, or all metapolycentric as in pea, [Macas 2023](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pgen.1010633)); the only published mixed case is *Morus notabilis*, whose polycentricity is inferred from CENH3 on all six chromosomes ([Ma 2023](https://doi.org/10.1093/hr/uhad111)). No study has compared, within one genome and one meiosis, recombination rate, TE accumulation, GC content and gene density between polycentric and monocentric chromosomes; the TE–centromere relationship also differs between systems (repeat-based centromere units distributed genome-wide in *Rhynchospora* vs satellite arrays embedded in an otherwise uniform 81.6 Mb pea CEN6). Within-genome comparisons of gene density and GC are reported only descriptively (gene-density minima at predicted centromeres in nettle; uniform gene density across pea CEN6).

**Open questions / gaps (Topic 12).** (i) Does the "canonical centromere effect" on crossovers exist on nettle's acrocentric chromosomes and vanish on its polycentric ones? No linkage map or pollen-sequencing study exists for *U. dioica*. (ii) Do the polycentric chromosomes of nettle carry Tyba-like genome-wide satellite units, Morus-like multi-site units, or pea-like extended domains? (iii) Is the chr8/chr13 fusion/fission associated with centromere type (chr8 and chr13 are both predicted acrocentric)? (iv) Whether TE density, GC content or gene density differ systematically between the two centromere classes within the nettle genome has not been tested in any publication.
